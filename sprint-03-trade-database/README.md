# Sprint 3: the trade database

Every other component in this platform is, in the end, a way of getting
something into this database or out of it. The Trade REST API in Sprint 6
writes orders to it. The Trade Executor in Sprint 7 updates them and moves
cash and holdings in one transaction. The pipeline in Sprint 7 extracts from
it. The Angular blotter in Sprint 9 renders what it returns. This is the
platform's memory, and it is the only part of the system whose contents are
the legal record of what a customer asked for and what happened next.

That makes two failure modes expensive. A schema that allows an impossible row
produces a record that has to be corrected by hand later: a negative holding,
an order against an account that was closed, two copies of the same submitted
order. A schema that is reshaped in Sprint 7 forces a rewrite of every mapper,
every query and every fixture written against it. You are designing for both:
correctness enforced by the database rather than hoped for in application
code, and a shape that survives the rest of the programme.

Nothing is given to you here. There is no schema file in `contracts/`,
deliberately, because the modelling is the assessment. What follows is the
domain. Turn it into a model.

## The domain

A retail brokerage runs the platform. Its customers hold cash with the firm,
buy and sell instruments through it, and expect to see what they own and what
they have done.

### Accounts

A customer trades through an account. An account holds a cash balance in a
single currency, carries the name of the holder, and is identified two ways:
by an internal key that the rest of the platform passes around, and by a
customer-facing reference that support staff and statements quote. Those two
are not the same thing and are not interchangeable.

An account is in exactly one of three states. `ACTIVE` accounts trade.
`SUSPENDED` accounts are frozen by compliance or by an unpaid balance: they can
be read, they cannot trade, and the suspension is reversible. `CLOSED`
accounts are finished. A closed account never trades again, but its history
must remain readable for years, so closing an account cannot mean deleting it.

Concurrency matters on this entity more than any other. Two orders arriving
at once for the same account both read the balance, both decide there is
enough cash, and both spend it. Your model needs a way for the second writer
to discover that it lost, rather than overwriting the first.

### Instruments

An instrument is something tradable: an equity, an exchange traded fund, a
currency pair, a crypto pair. Each has a symbol in the scheme the market-data
API uses, a display name, an asset class and a currency of quotation. Symbols
are externally assigned and stable, and the market-data API is the authority
on them.

An instrument can stop being tradable without ceasing to exist. A delisted
name still appears in years of order history, so removing the row is not an
option. The model must be able to refuse new orders in an instrument while
still resolving old ones.

### Orders

An order is a customer's instruction to buy or sell a quantity of one
instrument at a stated price, placed against one account. It is recorded when
it is received, before anyone knows whether it will succeed, because the
instruction itself is the thing the firm is legally on the hook for.

An order starts at `NEW`. From there it reaches exactly one terminal state:
`FILLED` when it executes, `REJECTED` when it cannot, `CANCELLED` when the
customer withdraws it before execution. Terminal means terminal. A filled
order is never reopened, and no state means "half filled": the Trade Executor
you build in Sprint 7 fills in full or rejects.

Rejected orders are kept. A customer who was refused four times last Tuesday
is a compliance question, and the answer has to be in the database.

Orders are the reason this is not an ordinary CRUD schema. They accumulate
without limit, they are read far more often than they are written, they are
never deleted, and they are the table every later sprint queries hardest.

### The idempotency key

A customer taps Buy. The response is slow, the phone drops the connection,
the app retries. Without a defence, the firm has bought twice.

The defence is a key generated by the client, sent with the order, and unique
across all orders forever. A retry carries the key of the request it is
retrying, so the second attempt is recognisable as the same instruction rather
than a new one.

Where you enforce that uniqueness decides whether it works. Reading first to
see whether the key exists, then inserting, is not enforcement: two concurrent
requests both read nothing and both insert. The database has to be the thing
that refuses, under a constraint, so that one of the two writers fails no
matter how the requests interleave. Sprint 6 depends on that failure being
detectable, so design for it now.

### Positions

A position is what an account currently holds in one instrument: a quantity
and the average price paid for it. An account holds each instrument at most
once, and quantities never go negative because short selling is out of scope.

Positions are derived state. Every position could in principle be rebuilt by
replaying the filled orders for that account, and a team that can demonstrate
that reconciliation understands its own model. They are stored anyway, because
the dashboard cannot afford to replay an order history on every page load.

### Historical trade data

The operational tables answer "what is true right now". The desk also asks
"what did we trade last quarter, by instrument, by week". Those are different
questions with different access patterns, and Sprint 4 builds a separate
analytical store for the second kind.

This sprint delivers the operational side of that boundary: structures that
retain the detail of what was executed, when, and at what achieved price, in a
form the Sprint 7 extract can pull incrementally without scanning the whole
history every night. Document how those structures are populated and how they
are queried. The design decision to record, and the reason for it, matters
more than the volume of rows you load.

### Money

Cash balances and prices are exact quantities in minor units. A binary
floating point balance cannot represent 0.10 and drifts by a few pence over a
few thousand trades, which is the kind of defect that surfaces in a
reconciliation six months later. Choose types that represent money exactly and
apply them consistently.

## What you deliver

| Deliverable | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| Numbered migration files creating the schema | `migrations/` |
| Seed data covering the states named below | `seed/` |
| An ER diagram every member of the team can walk | `design/er-diagram.md` or an exported image |
| Index justifications, one named query per index | `design/indexes.md` |
| A short data dictionary and your normalisation notes | `design/` |
| The manifest that tells the check harness your names | `manifest.env` |
| Two probe statements for the harness | `probes/` |

Create `design/` yourself. The harness checks that the diagram and the index
justifications exist, because a design with no written rationale cannot be
reviewed.

## How you work

Schema changes are migrations, not edits. Put each one in `migrations/` as a
`.sql` file numbered from `001_`, and apply the whole directory in filename
order with the provided script:

```bash
sprint-03-trade-database/scripts/apply.sh
```

That applies every migration, then everything in `seed/`, against the Postgres
container from the root `docker-compose.yml`. Start the infrastructure first
with `docker compose up -d`. To wipe the database and rebuild it from the
files:

```bash
sprint-03-trade-database/scripts/apply.sh --fresh
```

Run that often. A schema that only works because of something a teammate typed
into psql on Tuesday is a schema that will not survive the showcase.

Until your first design review, editing a migration in place is fine: you are
still converging. After the review, and for the rest of the programme, an old
migration is immutable. Change the schema by adding `002_`, `003_` and so on.
The reason is not ceremony. From Sprint 6 your teammates have databases with
data in them, and an edited `001_` never runs again on any of them.

From Sprint 6 the stack has to come up with the schema already in place. Copy
your migration and seed files into `infra/postgres/`, keeping the numbering,
so a fresh `docker compose up -d` builds the database unattended. See
`infra/postgres/README.md` for how that mount behaves.

## The queries your design has to serve

An index is not free. It is paid for on every insert and every update to the
columns it covers, and `orders` is the most write-heavy table you have. So no
index goes in without a query behind it, and these are the queries. Write them
out in SQL, run them against your seeded database, and read the plan with
`EXPLAIN ANALYZE` before and after you add the index.

| # | Query, in business terms | Who runs it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | All open orders for one account, newest first | The blotter, on every dashboard load |
| 2 | The last 50 orders for one account in any state, newest first | The order history screen |
| 3 | Everything one account currently holds, with quantity and average cost | The portfolio panel, on every dashboard load |
| 4 | Every order placed in one instrument between two dates, newest first | Desk exposure reporting |
| 5 | Every order created since a given timestamp, across all accounts | The nightly extract into the analytical store, Sprint 7 |
| 6 | Resolve an account from the customer-facing reference quoted on a call | Support, and the auth service at sign-in |
| 7 | Orders still in the working state, oldest first | The stuck-order sweep the Trade Executor needs, Sprint 7 |

Justify at least three indexes against three of these. A justification names
the query, states what the plan does without the index and with it, and says
what the index costs on write. "It seemed likely to help" is not a
justification.

Some of these queries need no new index, because a key you already declared
serves them. Write that down too, with the plan that proves it. It is a real
finding, and it is the cheapest index you will ever add. It does not count
towards the three: the three have to exist.

## The ER diagram

One diagram, committed to `design/`, showing every entity, its attributes,
its keys and the cardinality and optionality of every relationship. Two
formats are acceptable:

- Mermaid in a markdown file, `design/er-diagram.md`, using an `erDiagram`
  block. This is the better choice, because it diffs in review and it cannot
  drift out of the repository.
- An exported image, `design/er-diagram.png` or `.svg`, from whatever tool you
  drew it in. Commit the export, not a link to a cloud document that your
  instructor cannot open in the review.

Every member of the team walks the whole diagram unaided in the review. Not
the part they wrote. The whole diagram, including why a relationship is
optional and where a cardinality came from.

## Seed data

Seed data exists so that every query above returns something, and so that
Sprint 4 has data to analyse. It has to cover, at a minimum:

- accounts in all three states, `ACTIVE`, `SUSPENDED` and `CLOSED`
- an account with a balance too small to afford a realistic order
- several instruments, including at least one that is not an equity, and at
  least one that is no longer tradable
- orders spanning more than one lifecycle state, including at least one that
  was rejected
- holdings on more than one account, consistent with the filled orders that
  produced them

Use symbols the market-data API actually serves, so that quotes resolve in
Sprint 7 without a second pass over your fixtures.

## Acceptance criteria

These are the criteria your instructor assesses against.

1. The schema is in third normal form, with no redundant or transitive
   dependencies, and any deliberate denormalisation is documented with its
   reason.
2. The schema is version controlled as migration files, applied in a stable
   order, and the database can be rebuilt from those files alone.
3. Foreign keys and check constraints are present and enforce the domain
   rules, including a unique constraint on the order idempotency key.
4. At least three indexes exist and each is justified by one of the named
   queries.
5. Seed data loads with one command and covers active, suspended and closed
   accounts.
6. Structures for historical trade data exist, with their population and query
   path documented.
7. An ER diagram is committed, and every team member can walk it unaided.

One further criterion has no automated check and is worth more than the rest.
The model has to be the one the platform needs, not one that fits only this
week. From Sprint 5 your Java entities are built against it, in Sprint 6 your
mappers are written against it, and in Sprint 7 two services write to it
concurrently. Read `contracts/trade-api.yaml` while you design. It is the
contract the Sprint 6 service satisfies out of this database, and a schema
that cannot serve it is the wrong schema. Reading it is research, not
shortcutting.

## The check harness

`scripts/check.sh` asserts the things a machine can assert. Run it as often as
you like.

```bash
sprint-03-trade-database/scripts/check.sh
```

It creates a scratch database inside the same Postgres container, named
`trading_check` by default, applies every migration and every seed file to
that empty database, runs its assertions, and drops the scratch database when
they all pass. Your working `trading` database is never touched. When a check
fails the scratch database is left in place so you can look at it:

```bash
docker compose exec postgres psql -U postgres -d trading_check
```

Set `CHECK_DATABASE` in the environment to use a different name, and pass
`--keep` to hold the scratch database open after a run that passed.

What it asserts:

| Check | What it proves |
|---|---|
| `migrations/` holds numbered `.sql` files | The schema is version controlled |
| Every migration applies to an empty database, in order, without error | The database can be rebuilt from the files alone |
| Seed files apply after the migrations with one command | Criterion 5, first half |
| Accounts exist in all three states after seeding | Criterion 5, second half |
| A unique constraint covers the declared idempotency column | The constraint exists |
| A duplicate insert of that key is rejected by the database | The constraint is the enforcement, not application code |
| At least two foreign keys exist | The entities are related, not four flat tables |
| An insert referencing a parent that does not exist is rejected | The foreign keys are enforced |
| At least three check constraints exist, one covering account state | Domain rules live in the schema |
| At least three indexes exist that are not primary keys or constraint indexes | Criterion 4, the countable half |
| `design/` holds an ER diagram and an index justification file | Criteria 4 and 7, the reviewable half |

### How the harness avoids dictating your design

The harness cannot check a schema it has not seen without either guessing your
names or dictating them. So it asks. `manifest.env` in this folder is where
you declare the handful of names the harness needs: the accounts table, its
state column and the three literal values it stores, the orders table and the
column carrying the idempotency key. Fill it in once your first migration
exists. The harness reads it and adapts. It never asserts a name it was not
given, and it has no opinion about the rest of your schema.

Two assertions cannot be made from names alone, because only you know the
columns your tables require. Write them yourself, as SQL, in `probes/`:

- `probes/duplicate-idempotency-key.sql` inserts one valid order twice with
  the same idempotency key. The harness expects the second insert to fail with
  SQLSTATE `23505`, unique violation.
- `probes/orphan-foreign-key.sql` inserts one row that references a parent
  that does not exist. The harness expects SQLSTATE `23503`, foreign key
  violation.

Both run inside a transaction that is rolled back, so they leave nothing
behind, and both run after the seed data has loaded, so they can reference
seeded rows. Each file explains what to write in its own comments.

### What passing does not mean

Passing the harness is necessary and it is not sufficient. It cannot tell you
whether your schema is in third normal form, whether an index is justified or
merely present, whether your historical structures are the right shape, or
whether your team can explain any of it. Those are assessed by your instructor
in the design review, and the review is the assessment. A team can
pass every check here with a model that is quietly wrong in a way that costs
them a fortnight in Sprint 7.

Bring to the review: the ER diagram, your migrations, the seven queries with
their plans, your index justifications, and the answer to "why is it this way
and not the other way" for the three decisions you argued about the longest.
